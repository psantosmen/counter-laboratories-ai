Introduction

Ethics begins before the model is deployed

Debates about ethical artificial intelligence often begin with the behaviour of a finished system: Is it fair? Is it explainable? Is it safe? This project moves the point of departure upstream. It asks who produces AI, from which data and infrastructures, through whose labour, under what relations of ownership, and according to which visions of collective life.

The argument grows out of an ongoing multidisciplinary research project at UFRJ. It connects Actor-Network Theory and controversy mapping with critiques of surveillance capitalism, data colonialism and algorithmic rationality. Its central proposition is that ethical AI is not only a standard to be applied to systems after they exist. It is also a struggle over the material, institutional and epistemic conditions through which systems come into existence.

*

CORE THESIS

A system cannot be fully assessed as ethical if its outputs are evaluated while its supply chain, labour conditions, data ownership, ecological costs and geopolitical dependencies remain outside the frame.

01

Ethical AI is also a struggle over technological production

FROM PROPERTIES TO RELATIONS

Ethical AI is commonly defined through values that should guide the design and use of algorithmic systems: fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety, human agency and social benefit. They have created a shared language for identifying discrimination, demanding explanations and assigning responsibility. But they become politically thin when treated as properties of an isolated model rather than as questions about a distributed production system.

Contemporary AI is here understood as a sociotechnical assemblage. It depends on mineral extraction, energy and water; data centres and cloud contracts; scraped text, images and speech; standards and procurement rules; venture capital and public subsidies; content moderators, annotators and warehouse workers; programmers, researchers and affected communities. Each relation distributes visibility, risk, value and decision-making power. Ethical evaluation therefore has to follow the entire chain, not only the interface where a user meets a model.

This reframing changes the central question. Instead of asking only whether an AI system behaves responsibly, we ask how the system organizes social relations. Who can refuse data collection? Who can inspect or modify the infrastructure? Who performs necessary but hidden work? Which languages and forms of knowledge become legible to the model? Which communities receive benefits, and which absorb environmental or political costs?

Three families of institutional language

The terms ethical AI, responsible AI and trustworthy AI overlap, but they are not identical. They emphasize different moments in the passage from values to institutions and technical practice.

Term Primary question Illustrative institutional formulations
Ethical AI Which values, rights and visions of the good should orient AI? Stanford HAI (United States); Cambridge PACE (United Kingdom); UNESCO (international, based in France); Digital Cooperation Organization (intergovernmental, based in Saudi Arabia).
Responsible AI Which actors must implement safeguards, document decisions and answer for harms? NIST AI Risk Management Framework (United States); corporate governance programmes such as Pega Responsible AI (United States); ISO/IEC standards (international).
Trustworthy AI Which qualities and evidence make reliance on an AI system warranted? European Commission High-Level Expert Group on AI (European Union, Brussels); OECD AI Principles (international, based in France).

02

What conventional ethical frameworks leave outside

THE ISSUE IS NOT ABSENCE, BUT POLITICAL FRAMING

A strong critique should not claim that mainstream frameworks simply ignore labour, sustainability or human rights. UNESCO, the European Union and the OECD address these themes to varying degrees. Structural conditions often appear as external impacts to be assessed or risks to be managed, while the ownership and organization of technological production remain comparatively stable.

This produces a recurrent narrowing: ethics becomes a layer of principles, checklists, audits and compliance added to systems whose supply chains and business models are taken for granted. The system may be made more explainable without making its data infrastructure more democratic; less biased without changing who owns the model; safer for the user without improving the conditions of the workers who label its data.

Six structural blind spots

  • Extraction and ownership. Where data comes from, whether collection is consensual, and who can profit from or govern the resulting asset.

  • Invisible and segmented labour. Annotation, moderation, logistics, maintenance and creative work distributed through unequal contracts and geographies.

  • Infrastructure and dependency. Reliance on cloud providers, proprietary models, chips, standards and platforms controlled by a small number of firms and states.

  • Epistemic and linguistic inequality. The elevation of dominant languages and classifications over local knowledge, cultural plurality and community-defined categories.

  • Material and ecological costs. Energy, water, land, minerals, electronic waste and the unequal territorial distribution of environmental burdens.

  • Collective power and refusal. The ability of workers and communities to govern, redesign, contest or reject a system—not merely to receive notice or appeal an output.

Global reviews of AI ethics guidelines have found convergence around transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility and privacy, alongside substantial divergence about what those principles mean and how they should be implemented (Jobin, Ienca, and Vayena, 2019). Mittelstadt (2019) similarly argues that high-level principles cannot by themselves guarantee ethical practice. A technopolitical approach adds a further point: implementation is not only an engineering problem. It is a conflict over institutions, property and collective capacity.

*

ANALYTICAL SHIFT

We do not ask only whether ethics is present in a framework. We ask whether structural questions are treated as negotiable relations of power—or merely as impacts surrounding an otherwise fixed model of production.

03

Defining counter-laboratory

A RELATIONAL DEFINITION

The idea of counter-laboratories is based on Latour’s actor-network analysis (2000). Latour highlights the importance of studying so-called scientific controversies. These controversies can lead to the establishment of new research hubs and laboratories—what he terms "counter-laboratories." In this view, every laboratory is essentially a counter-laboratory born of politico-scientific controversies, proposing alternative lines of interpretation for the same phenomenon. Such scientific controversies, therefore, take on a political dimension. In the case of Artificial Intelligence, ongoing controversies stem from philosophical and sociological critiques regarding the North-centric and capital-centric nature of system development; these critiques emphasize the need to consider "technodiversity"—that is, the diverse worldviews of different peoples that could lead to the development of alternative forms of AI. These controversies have ultimately given rise to various "counter-laboratories" or initiatives by social movements aimed at the reappropriation of data and programming.

We define a counter-laboratory as an experimental sociotechnical space that emerges from controversy and develops the collective capacity to contest, investigate, and reconfigure dominant technological arrangements. It may be an organization, a project, a network, a campaign or an infrastructure. Its defining feature is not its legal form or whether it calls itself a laboratory. It is what the arrangement is able to do.

The concept combines four dimensions. First, a counter-laboratory opens a dispute that dominant institutions have attempted to close—for example, the claim that mass extraction is an unavoidable condition of AI. Second, it assembles actors and instruments: communities, researchers, datasets, software, legal strategies, workshops, maps, archives and funding. Third, it produces rival inscriptions such as audits, community maps, open models, testimonies, policy proposals and prototypes. Fourth, it experiments with alternative relations of ownership, governance, care and knowledge.

An initiative becomes analytically relevant as a counter-laboratory when several of the following are observable:

  • It identifies a concrete sociotechnical controversy rather than invoking innovation in the abstract.

  • It produces knowledge, evidence or artifacts that affected groups can use.

  • It changes who is authorized to define the problem and evaluate the solution.

  • It makes material infrastructures, labour or data relations visible and contestable.

  • It prototypes, maintains or governs an alternative—not only a critique.

  • It sustains collective participation and accountability beyond a one-off event.

This is not a rigid scorecard. Some initiatives primarily contest and document; others build and maintain technical systems. The counter-laboratory is best understood as a relational capacity that can be strong in one operation and weak in another. The concept should therefore be used comparatively and revised through fieldwork.

04

From scientific dissent to technopolitical experimentation

OPENING BLACK BOXES

In Science in Action, Bruno Latour follows scientific claims before they become stabilized facts. Controversies reveal the people, instruments, inscriptions, resources and alliances required to make a statement durable. A dissenting group cannot oppose a laboratory through opinion alone: it has to assemble a counter-laboratory capable of producing alternative evidence and attracting allies.

Applied to AI, this insight expands beyond scientific debate. The relevant black box is not only the model. It includes datasets, interfaces, procurement arrangements, energy systems, moderation queues, legal classifications, benchmarks and narratives of inevitability. A counter-laboratory is technopolitical because it intervenes simultaneously in technical design and in the distribution of authority.

Methodology: Controversy mapping and network visualization provides a method for following these heterogeneous actors without deciding in advance which ones matter. Algorithms, platforms and infrastructures enter the map alongside experts, public officials, workers, activists and affected communities. Venturini's approach stresses that maps should adapt to the object, combine multiple accounts and remain reversible: the reader must be able to move between an overview and the underlying evidence (Venturini, 2012; Venturini and Munk, 2022).

From critique to technical opening

This project also draws on Gilbert Simondon to resist an exclusively closed view of technology. Technical objects contain indeterminacies; they can be displaced from the purposes and environments for which they were originally designed. In this reading, algorithms are not finished essences but processes whose trajectories can be reopened and connected to collective struggles (Corrêa and Cocco, 2024).

Counter-laboratories occupy this opening. They do not presume that every dominant technology can be redeemed, nor that technical invention automatically produces emancipation. They test where refusal is necessary, where appropriation is possible, and where entirely different infrastructures must be built. The result is less a single alternative AI than a field of situated experiments.

05

Six operations of contemporary counter-laboratories

AN ANALYTICAL VOCABULARY FOR COMPARISON

The initiatives in this research can be compared through six operations. An organization may perform several at once, and the balance can change over time. These operations make the concept empirical: each can be connected to observable practices, artifacts and relations.

1 · CONTEST

Open controversies that dominant actors present as settled. Challenge claims of neutrality, inevitability and universal benefit through advocacy, litigation, public campaigns or critical research.

2 · MAKE VISIBLE

Trace hidden labour, surveillance, data flows, territorial harms, funding or infrastructure. Produce audits, maps, archives, reports and testimonies that render a supply chain publicly debatable.

3 · RECLAIM

Recover control over data, code, technical capacity, representation and institutional agendas. Reclamation can involve open-source tools, community archives, data stewardship or worker-controlled platforms.

4 · SITUATE

Design from particular languages, territories, histories and embodied experiences. Situated practice rejects the assumption that categories and benchmarks developed in dominant centres are universally adequate.

5 · EXPERIMENT

Prototype alternative datasets, interfaces, models, infrastructures and governance arrangements. Experimentation treats ethics as a method of production rather than an external review stage.

6 · COLLECTIVIZE

Redistribute expertise, ownership and decision-making. Build durable forms of participation, mutual support, maintenance and care so that affected groups are co-producers rather than consulted users.

*

IMPORTANT

A project may open data while depending on corporate cloud infrastructure; a coalition may contest regulation without building technology. Contradictions belong inside the map.

06

Counter-laboratories are not all the same kind of actor

AVOIDING A FLAT CATALOGUE

A list of initiatives can create the illusion that every entry is comparable. In fact, the research object includes organizations, campaigns, laboratories, educational programmes, software projects, models, datasets, coalitions and directories. Treating them as a single class would erase differences in scale, durability, governance and technical capacity.

The unit of analysis should therefore be explicit. Open Knowledge Brasil is an organization that hosts multiple projects; Querido Diário is one of its civic infrastructures. PretaLab is an Olabi initiative; Conecta PretaLab is a recurring programme and discursive space. TybyrIA is a model and dataset produced within the Código Não Binário network. The AI Resist List is a curated directory, not an organization equivalent to the groups it documents.

Brazil as a field of sociotechnical experimentation

A DENSE ECOLOGY OF DATA, MOVEMENTS AND PUBLIC PROBLEMS

Brazil is a field in which acute racial, gendered, territorial and labour inequalities coexist with traditions of free software, open government data, community communication, social-movement organizing and digital-rights advocacy. This combination makes visible both the violence of platform infrastructures and the capacity to invent alternatives from situated problems.

The cases below can all be called AI counter-laboratories. Their relevance is precisely that they redistribute different components of AI production: data, technical skills, public narratives, territorial representation, legal power, worker coordination and institutional alliances. 08

Resisting AI

INTERNATIONAL PROJECTS MAPPING RESISTANCE TO AI

Resistance to artificial intelligence is becoming an emerging object of research, documentation and public intervention. A growing number of international projects are collecting evidence about how workers, communities, artists, researchers and civil-society organisations contest the development, infrastructures and uses of AI. These projects differ in scope: some assemble examples intended to inspire action, while others produce systematic datasets, analytical classifications or sector-specific mappings.

The AI Resist List is one of the broadest public attempts to document this emerging field. It presents resistance not simply as opposition to technology, but as action against the material and institutional “pillars” supporting dominant AI systems: narratives, funding, data, data centres, resource extraction, labour, adoption, surveillance and policy. Its repertoire includes litigation, strikes, public campaigns, data poisoning, technological refusal, community-controlled infrastructures and the construction of alternative futures.

However, its geographical coverage remains highly uneven. As of September 2026, a manual count identifies 31 initiatives in the Resist List itself. Only three are located in Latin America, including just one in Brazil: the campaign against Google’s data centre in Uruguay; Quili.ai in Chile; and O Panóptico in Brazil. A separate section on “Possible Futures” includes Hire a COOP, a transnational initiative involving cooperatives in Brazil and Argentina, but it is not part of the main Resist List. This limited presence is significant because the project explicitly aims to prioritise the Global Majority. It suggests that the public visibility of resistance remains shaped by linguistic, institutional and informational asymmetries, rather than necessarily reflecting the actual density of mobilisation in these territories.

A more specialised and systematic initiative is Worker Mobilizations around AI in Arts, Culture, and Media, developed within the Creative Labour and Critical Futures research cluster at the University of Toronto Scarborough. The tracker documents 142 worker organisations in 34 countries and eight cultural sectors, recording their arguments, repertoires of action, negotiations, agreements and other outcomes. Latin America accounts for 11 per cent of the organisations mapped. Its focus is narrower than that of the AI Resist List, but its data structure, source documentation and participatory verification provide a stronger basis for comparative research into worker-led AI governance. The project is itself included in the AI Resist List, demonstrating the connections already emerging between these different mapping efforts.

Another relevant academic initiative is the Algorithmic Collective Action Repository, developed through research at the ELLIS Institute Tübingen. It currently documents 126 real-world cases and references 49 research papers and related initiatives. Rather than mapping every form of resistance to AI, it examines how groups act collectively within algorithmically mediated systems. Its taxonomy distinguishes interventions across stages of the AI lifecycle—including data collection, training, deployment and evaluation—and classifies how participants acquire knowledge, coordinate action and attempt to influence algorithmic systems. The repository also explicitly acknowledges the over-representation of Europe and North America and the comparative absence of South America, Africa, the Middle East and other regions.

The Sciences Po research paper From Rejection to Regulation: Mapping the Landscape of AI Resistance provides a complementary analytical approach. Rather than maintaining a living database, it compares resistance across six fields: creative industries, migration and borders, healthcare, higher education, defence and security, and environmental activism. It identifies five major drivers of resistance: socio-economic consequences, ethical harms, safety concerns, threats to democracy and sovereignty, and environmental impacts. The study is particularly useful for situating individual mobilisations within a broader conceptual and political framework.

Taken together, these projects reveal a field that is expanding but remains fragmented. The AI Resist List maps a broad repertoire of resistance; the Creative Labour and Critical Futures tracker offers sectoral depth and structured evidence; the Algorithmic Collective Action Repository focuses on collective interventions within algorithmic systems; and the Sciences Po study develops a cross-sectoral analytical framework. Yet none provides a dense account of the Brazilian field, and Latin American experiences remain comparatively under-represented.

The Counter-Laboratories for the AI Commons project responds to this gap by identifying 44 initiatives in Brazil alone. It asks not only how actors oppose particular AI systems, but also how they produce knowledge, reclaim data and technical capacity, construct alternative infrastructures and experiment with collective forms of technological governance. In this sense, Brazil does not appear as a peripheral recipient of international debates, but as a dense field of sociotechnical resistance and experimentation.

09

Next steps: mapping controversies and pedagogies of the AI commons

FROM DOCUMENTING INITIATIVES TO FOLLOWING DISPUTES, CREATING ALTERNATIVE VISUALITIES AND PRODUCING SHARED KNOWLEDGE

The next phase of the research will move beyond identifying and classifying initiatives. It will examine how counter-laboratories intervene in controversies, how these disputes can be represented through alternative forms of mapping, and how their practices may contribute to the development of pedagogies of the AI commons.

1. Producing a controversy mapping

The next step will be to identify how the initiatives contest Big Tech companies, government institutions and dominant technological arrangements. This involves examining not only which actors are criticised, but also what precisely is at stake in each dispute: data extraction, surveillance, labour exploitation, environmental impacts, automated discrimination, platform dependency, epistemic inequalities or the concentration of technological power.

In this research, controversy is not restricted to disagreements within the scientific field. The concept is extended to sociotechnical disputes in which different actors struggle to define a problem, establish relevant evidence, represent affected groups and determine which technological futures are considered possible. A controversy may therefore involve social movements, researchers, workers, communities, governments, companies, technical systems, legal instruments and material infrastructures.

2. Mapping as a technical-aesthetic artifact

The second step will treat mapping not merely as a method for displaying research results, but as a technical-aesthetic artifact. Conventional network visualisations frequently reproduce the visual language of automated systems: centralised graphs, data-driven hierarchies and seemingly objective representations produced through computational abstraction.

The project will experiment with mappings that are less governed by AI-generated classifications and automated layouts, while still making complex relations accessible. This may involve combining digital visualisation with situated descriptions, manually curated pathways, narrative maps, collective annotation and other forms of visual composition.

3. Deepening the idea of pedagogies of the AI commons

The third step concerns the theoretical development of pedagogies of the AI commons. Counter-laboratories do more than criticise dominant AI systems. Through workshops, investigations, community technologies, collective archives, public campaigns and experimental infrastructures, they produce knowledge about how AI works, whom it benefits and how it might be organised differently.

Pedagogies of the AI commons can be understood as practices through which communities collectively learn to investigate, contest, appropriate, redesign or refuse AI systems. They connect critical literacy with the capacity to act: opening technical black boxes, making infrastructures and labour visible, recognising situated forms of knowledge and creating shared criteria for technological decision-making.

This perspective also expands the meaning of AI literacy. Instead of preparing individuals only to adapt to existing systems, it asks how educational practices can strengthen collective agency over data, infrastructures, technical knowledge and technological futures. Learning about AI thus becomes inseparable from learning how to participate in its governance—and how to imagine forms of computation grounded in democratic control, care, social justice and epistemic diversity.

10

From a catalogue of initiatives to a multilayer network

MAPPING ASSOCIATIONS WITHOUT INVENTING TIES

A network visualization should not begin by connecting organizations merely because they share a theme. Semantic proximity is not the same as a social relation. The map must preserve the evidence for every edge and distinguish between declared collaboration, technical dependency, hyperlink citation, shared participation and the researcher's own analytical coding.

Layers of the proposed network

Actors

Organizations, movements, research groups, funders, public bodies, companies and communities.

Projects and artifacts

Platforms, models, datasets, maps, reports, campaigns, workshops, archives and legal actions.

Operations

Contest, make visible, reclaim, situate, experiment and collectivize.

Controversies

Data extraction, platform labour, surveillance, racism, gender violence, language, territory, ecology, public governance and dependency.

Geographies

Cities, states, countries, territories and transnational South-South relations.

Resources and infrastructure

Funding, cloud services, repositories, communication platforms, standards, public data and community facilities.

Pilati and Venturini's recent web mapping of AI initiatives against disinformation illustrates the value and the limits of hyperlink networks. Their map used 81 nodes and 393 links, Gephi and ForceAtlas2, followed by manual qualitative interpretation. The authors also identify structural underrepresentation of non-Western and civil-society initiatives. For the present project, that limitation is not noise to remove; it is itself a controversy about visibility and infrastructural inequality.

*

CARTOGRAPHIC PRINCIPLE

The map is an interface for navigating disagreement, not a final picture of the field. It should be plural, traceable and reversible: every pattern must lead back to actors, documents and the conditions of data collection.

11

AI commons emerges from alternative modes of production

ETHICS AS INFRASTRUCTURE

The counter-laboratory perspective does not reject fairness, transparency, accountability or safety. It relocates them. These values are not only qualities to verify at the end of a development pipeline; they must be built into decisions about what is produced, which data is collected, how labour is organized, who owns the infrastructure and who can govern or refuse the system.

This is why community mapping, feminist digital care, open civic data, worker-controlled platforms and Black or LGBTQIA+ technical education belong in a conversation about ethical AI even when they do not train large models. They intervene in the social capacities and infrastructures from which AI futures are made. They turn affected groups from objects of protection into producers of knowledge, standards and technical arrangements.

Yuk Hui's idea of technodiversity helps name the horizon: technological development should not converge on a single cosmology, scale or institutional form. 'Algorithms of the commons' likewise suggests that algorithms can be treated as open and unfinished technical beings, embedded in processes of collective individuation rather than sealed within proprietary platforms. Counter-laboratories give these propositions an empirical site.

They do not offer one pure substitute for corporate AI. They are partial, hybrid and sometimes dependent on the infrastructures they criticize. Their importance lies in making alternatives practicable and contestable: localized datasets, community stewardship, open tools, collective governance, slower forms of technical work, cultural plurality and the right to refuse.

*

CONCLUSION

AI commons is not only safer computation. It is the democratic reorganization of the conditions of computation—data, labour, infrastructure, knowledge, ecology and power.

Seen from this perspective, the task of research is double. It must reveal how dominant AI supply chains are assembled, and it must follow the fragile networks in which other forms of technological production are already being tested. The proposed multilayer atlas can make that field visible without freezing it: a map of controversies, experiments and alliances through which more socially sustainable, politically accountable and epistemically diverse AI futures may be composed.
